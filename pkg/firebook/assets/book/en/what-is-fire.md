# What FIRE actually is
<!-- source: fire-cest-quoi @ 9d73e3fec2ed -->

FIRE stands for *Financial Independence, Retire Early*.

Behind the acronym sits an old and simple idea: build enough capital that its return covers what you spend, then live on that capital, with or without a job, at an age you choose rather than one the law chooses for you. This page maps the subject. Where the movement came from, what its variants mean (Lean, Fat, Barista, Coast), the ballpark numbers that shape everything else, and what this book will teach you to do.

By the end you will know where your own project sits on that map, and which article to read next.

::: cle The principle in one sentence
When your invested wealth reaches roughly 25 to 33 times your annual spending, its real return (after inflation) can fund your life indefinitely, with high probability but never with certainty. Everything else in this field, and everything in this book, comes down to understanding that sentence, refining it, securing it and living it.
:::

## Where it came from

Living off capital is as old as capital, but the modern FIRE movement has a precise family tree.

**1992: *Your Money or Your Life***. Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez's book lays the philosophical base: money is life energy traded away, every purchase can be priced in hours of your life, and financial independence is the point where investment income crosses spending. No withdrawal rate yet, but already the founding chart: two curves, spending and passive income, and the crossing that sets you free.

**1994: William Bengen**. A California financial planner publishes, in the *Journal of Financial Planning*, the article that invents the safe withdrawal rate. He replays every US retirement since 1926. An initial withdrawal of 4% of capital, adjusted for inflation each year, survived every 30-year period, including the ones that started in 1929 and in 1966. The 4% rule is born ([[the-trinity-study]]).

**1998: the Trinity study** confirms the result and popularizes it, with a grid of success rates by withdrawal rate, allocation and horizon.

**2011: Mr. Money Mustache**. Pete Adeney's blog turns financial-planning literature into a cultural movement: an engineer retired at 30, a provocative voice, and a piece of arithmetic that ties your savings rate to the working years you have left (see the table below). The FIRE community explodes into forums, blogs, podcasts and subreddits.

**2016 and after: scientific maturity**. Karsten Jeske's *Early Retirement Now* ([[the-ern-series]]) takes the subject up with an economist's rigor and dismantles the first wave's shortcuts: the 4% rule does not fit a 50-year retirement, starting valuations matter enormously, flexibility has limits. Academics widen the sample beyond the United States at the same time ([[anarkulova-cederburg]]), and institutional practitioners (Morningstar, Vanguard, pension funds) publish frameworks of their own ([[morningstar-guardrails]], [[expected-returns]]).

::: encart Where the movement stands today
The FIRE of 2026 is not the FIRE of 2012. The first wave sold a certainty: 4%, question settled. The state of the art now sells a method: model the uncertainty honestly, pick an adaptive withdrawal rule, build a portfolio that holds up in hostile regimes, and keep margins. That is exactly this book's program.
:::

## The variants: Lean, Fat, Barista, Coast

The movement produced a vocabulary for very different projects that all run on the same machinery.

| Variant | The idea | Spending | Who it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lean FIRE** | Deliberate frugality, minimum capital | EUR 15,000 to 25,000 a year | Single, or a thrifty couple; home paid off or a low-cost country |
| **Classic FIRE** | A normal life without a job | EUR 25,000 to 45,000 a year | A disciplined middle-income household |
| **Fat FIRE** | Comfort with no compromises | EUR 60,000 a year and well beyond | High earners, founders, late-career executives |
| **Barista FIRE** | Partial capital plus a job you actually want | Capital covers 50 to 80% of spending | Leaves the main job early, tops it up with work they enjoy |
| **Coast FIRE** | The capital is already enough... eventually | Nothing is withdrawn: it is left to compound | "Done saving" at 35, works to cover current spending until 50 or 60 |

Two things about that table matter.

First, **the difficulty is not linear**. Going from Lean to Fat does not merely triple the target capital. It changes the nature of the problem. A Lean FIRE at EUR 20,000 a year has enormous levers (geographic arbitrage, a spell back at work, [[going-back-to-work]]) because a minimum-wage job covers its spending. A Fat FIRE at EUR 100,000 a year cannot make up a shortfall with a part-time job: its safety has to sit entirely inside the plan.

Second, **Barista and Coast are sequence-risk absorbers**, not just lifestyles. A few years of partial income at the start of retirement cut withdrawals sharply during the most dangerous window ([[sequence-of-returns]]). Mathematically that is one of the most powerful tools in the field, and [[pensions-and-other-income]] comes back to it.

## The arithmetic that makes FIRE possible

What drives FIRE is not return, it is the **savings rate**, and it works on two fronts at once: saving more speeds up the accumulation and lowers the target, since you are proving you can live on less. Hence the famous table Mr. Money Mustache popularized, computed with a 5% real return, a 4% withdrawal and a start from zero:

| Savings rate | Working years to independence |
|---|---|
| 10% | ~51 years |
| 20% | ~37 years |
| 35% | ~25 years |
| 50% | ~17 years |
| 65% | ~10.5 years |
| 80% | ~5.5 years |

::: exemple One couple, in numbers
Léa and Sam, both 32, take home EUR 5,400 a month between them and spend EUR 3,200. Savings rate: 41%. Target at 4%: 3,200 × 12 × 25 = EUR 960,000. With EUR 90,000 already invested and EUR 2,200 a month going in at 5% real, they reach the target around 50; at 4% real, around 52. Cut spending by EUR 400 a month and the target falls to EUR 840,000 while savings climb to EUR 2,600 a month: independence moves three to four years closer. That is the two-front effect. For the target calculation itself, see [[how-much-you-need]].
:::

::: attention Return will not save you
The classic beginner's temptation: make up for thin savings with an "aggressive" portfolio. Over 15 years of accumulation, taking expected return from 5% to 7% buys 2 to 3 years, when all goes well, and exposes you to far worse sequences. Taking your savings rate from 20% to 35% buys 12 years, with no luck involved. The order of priorities is not ambiguous: spending first, then income, and only then portfolio optimization.
:::

## What FIRE is not

A few points the first wave of the movement sometimes left fuzzy.

**It is not "never working again."** Most real FIRE retirees earn something after they quit: a project that turned lucrative, occasional consulting, a hobby that started paying. Not because the plan failed, but because people who can save 50% of their income for 15 years are rarely the type to stop producing ([[meaning-and-identity]]).

**It is not a guarantee.** Every withdrawal rate is a probability, not a promise. "95% historical success" means that in 1 case out of 20 in the American past the plan failed, and the future is under no obligation to look like the American past ([[simulator-traps]]). What a FIRE plan really delivers is not a number. It is a number **plus** an adjustment strategy ([[withdrawal-strategies-overview]]) **plus** margins ([[cash-buffer]]).

**It is not reserved for engineers making $200,000 a year.** The framework works at any level of spending; only the timeline changes. What does differ from one country to the next is the plumbing around it: which accounts shelter your investments, how the withdrawal phase is taxed, and when a state pension finally arrives to help ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]]).

**It is not deprivation.** The movement has an ascetic wing, but the durable version of FIRE optimizes the cost of the life *you want to live*, not the cheapest life available. A plan built on artificially crushed spending fails in the most human way there is: nobody sticks to it ([[the-psychology-of-spending]]).

## The three numbers that govern everything

Whatever the variant, every FIRE project comes down to three numbers and their uncertainties.

**1. Your target annual spending.** The most important number, and the one people get most wrong. Not what you spend today: what the life you are aiming at costs, health care, taxes and the smoothing of big irregular expenses included ([[spending-in-retirement]]).

**2. Your withdrawal rate.** The bridge between spending and capital. 4% is the historical starting point ([[the-4-percent-rule]]). With an early start, a global portfolio and high valuations, the modern working range is closer to 3 to 3.5% for a rigid withdrawal, and more than that with a flexible strategy ([[choosing-your-strategy]]).

**3. Your horizon.** Thirty years (what the founding studies assumed) and fifty years (leaving at 40) are two different problems. A long horizon lowers the safe rate, but less than you would think. Past 40 years the curve flattens. A portfolio that survives 40 years has almost always grown so large that it survives forever ([[horizon-and-life-expectancy]]).

::: astuce Start with the inventory, not with the simulator
Before any clever calculation: twelve months of bank statements, a spreadsheet, and the true figure for your annual spending, big irregular items included (car, home repairs, health, gifts). Most people find a gap of 15 to 25% between what they think they spend and what they spend. That one number, multiplied by 25 or 30, tells you where you stand better than every simulator in the world.
:::

## How to read this book

The book is built of self-contained articles, densely linked to each other. Start wherever your situation points you.

- **New to the subject**: go on to [[the-4-percent-rule]], then [[how-much-you-need]], and keep [[ten-plan-wrecking-mistakes]] handy as a guardrail.
- **You want the science**: "The science of withdrawal" reads in order, from [[the-trinity-study]] to [[the-ern-series]]; the conceptual pivot is [[sequence-of-returns]].
- **You are close to leaving**: withdrawal strategies ([[withdrawal-strategies-overview]]), the portfolio ([[stock-bond-allocation]], [[all-weather-portfolios]]) and the protections ([[cash-buffer]], [[the-bucket-strategy]]) are your chapters.
- **You want the local framework**: "Taxes and the US framework" ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]], [[us-taxes-in-the-withdrawal-phase]], [[us-healthcare-and-social-security]]) covers the accounts, the tax on the withdrawal phase, health cover before Medicare, and Social Security.
- **You are using the simulator**: [[using-the-fire-simulator]] walks through every control of the FIRE simulator, and [[under-the-hood]] shows what happens beneath it. This book points at that simulator now and then, because it was built while the book was being written and it is free to use, but other excellent ones (FICalc, cFIREsim, TPAW Planner) are worth your time too.

::: terrain What the people who did it repeat most
Ask anyone who has actually left (forums, blogs, meetups) and one piece of advice comes back more than any other: "I should have spent less time optimizing my withdrawal rate to the tenth of a point, and more time preparing what I was going to do with my days." The financial machinery is the easy part; the hard part is human ([[voices-from-real-retirees]], [[meaning-and-identity]]). This book covers both, in that order, but do not skip the second.
:::
