# What real FIRE retirees say: accounts and advice
<!-- source: temoignages-fire @ 185e15ee808c -->

Sixty chapters of models in, here is the common-sense question. What do the people who actually **did** it say? The FIRE movement is twenty years old and has one precious property: it writes. Blogs kept during and after the exit, annual reviews published with the numbers attached, forums where veterans answer the candidates, and a handful of long accounts that have become canonical: the decumulation diary of "LivingAFI", the reviews Early Retirement Now has published since its author quit in 2018, and the twenty years of hindsight of Mr. Money Mustache.

This chapter pulls it together. Method first, because these accounts carry massive biases and they are worth naming. Then the **six** findings that come back in almost every story: money (it is almost never the problem), work, time, the social side, anxiety and regret. The first of those regrets will surprise anyone who has not read [[one-more-year]]. Last, the canonical list of advice the veterans repeat to the candidates. This is the book's field chapter. It does not replace the models. It says what the models cannot see.

::: cle Method first: reading these accounts without being fooled
Three biases shape the whole corpus. First, **survivorship**. The people who publish reviews are the ones it worked for; the failures go quiet or vanish from the forums. Correct for it by hunting down the accounts of failure and of going back to work: they exist, and they are worth the search. Second, **selection**. Among FIRE bloggers, high-earning American engineers are wildly over-represented; a reader with an ordinary plan transposes, never copies. Third, **narrative**. A life story smooths itself into a coherent arc, and the years of drift turn into "my transition". So read the diaries written as things happened, the most honest part of the corpus, rather than the five-year retrospectives.
:::

## Finding 1: the money works (and it is not the issue)

This is the largest finding in the corpus, and the most reassuring. Among people who left with a serious plan (a sane starting rate, real margins), **the financial machinery works, and usually better than planned**. The published year-by-year numbers tell the same story every time. Spending comes in under plan first, the underspending of [[the-psychology-of-spending]], nearly universal in the early years. Then income keeps arriving without being chased, the most counter-intuitive finding for a candidate: projects that get monetized, work that gets offered, hobbies that pay, because skill does not switch off with the badge. And portfolios that, with the 2010s behind them, grew. The flexibility everyone theorizes about ([[flexibility-in-practice]]) was barely ever used in anger; 2020 and 2022 produced comfort adjustments, not floors. The sample deserves its warning: one decade, a friendly one, and the 2000 and 1966 vintages have no bloggers ([[simulator-traps]]). But the finding holds. In the real accounts, **a FIRE crisis is almost never financial**. It lives in the five findings that follow.

## Finding 2: almost nobody really stops producing

The corpus is unanimous to the point of comedy. Forty-year-old "retirees" build houses, write books, start blogs that turn into businesses, consult, teach. The forums' retirement police calls that income a betrayal of the concept, and misses what every one of them points out. **FIRE does not remove work; it removes the obligation to work.** Work you choose, dose and can walk away from is a different animal ([[going-back-to-work]], [[meaning-and-identity]]). For the plan, the consequence is already built into this book. Income after the exit is likely but never **counted**; it is margin ([[how-much-you-need]]). The accounts confirm both halves. That income almost always shows up, and the people who **needed** it to show up spent their first years in dread.

## Finding 3: time, the honeymoon, then the eighteen-month wall

This is the most repeated time pattern in the corpus. The first six to twelve months are euphoric: rest, the backlog of projects, the list of things you always meant to do. Then the list runs out. Somewhere between month 12 and month 24 comes what the accounts call the wall, the trough, the "now what?". Days lose their structure, motivation refuses to be summoned, and sometimes a real depression settles in. LivingAFI's diary is still the most honest chronicle of it, even though his own trough did not arrive until the third year; his return to work, four and a half years after leaving, came down to something else entirely (a separation and a medical diagnosis that redid the math for one). The pattern matches what the research observes outside FIRE, the honeymoon-then-disenchantment sequence of Atchley's phase model (1976) and the U-shaped trough that Wang's cohorts (2007) measure in about one retiree in four ([[meaning-and-identity]]). The veterans converge on the diagnosis. You do not retire from something, you retire toward something. And the "toward" cannot be improvised on arrival: it gets prototyped beforehand ([[the-three-phases]], the trial sabbatical, [[meaning-and-identity]] for the substance). The ones who get through it best either had a practice that already gave the week a shape (an art, a sport, a commitment) or the sense to treat year one as **convalescence** and year two as construction. End-of-career burnout is over-represented among FIRE retirees.

## Finding 4: your social life gets rebuilt, it does not survive on its own

This is the finding every candidate underestimates. Work was quietly supplying most of the daily social contact: colleagues, rituals, belonging. And at 45, **everyone** works. Friends are unavailable on weekdays, the gap in rhythms sets in ("they talk promotions, I talk hiking"), and new ties do not form by themselves in a world organized around employment. The couples add a chapter of their own ([[couples-and-family]]). One partner free while the other is still at the office is a recurring source of friction; so is the wall-to-wall togetherness of a synchronized exit. What works, in the accounts? Communities of interest with real responsibilities rather than a walk-on part: clubs, associations, serious volunteering. FIRE communities too, meetups and forums, the only places where your situation is normal. And deliberate investment: the veterans talk about their social life as **a line in the plan**, as seriously as asset allocation.

## Finding 5: financial anxiety fades, slowly and through experience

The accounts about money trace a typical curve. Year one is compulsive portfolio checking and guilt at every purchase ([[the-psychology-of-spending]] is the living illustration). In years 2 and 3, the first market drop **lived through** without damage is worth every simulation put together: the −15% arrives, the plan holds, nothing happens. Confidence is built by the test, not by the spreadsheet. From year four on, most veterans report looking at the portfolio quarterly at most, and having raised their standard of living. One last marker of maturity comes up often: they have stopped identifying with their withdrawal rate. The lesson for a candidate? The anxiety of the early years is **normal** and temporary. Equip it (the automatic paycheck, the cash buffer, [[the-psychology-of-spending]]) and let experience do the rest.

::: terrain The number one regret, unanimously
Ask the veterans what they most regret. One answer crushes every other, on every forum, in every language: **"I should have left sooner"**. Not "I should have saved more", not "I should have picked a smarter portfolio". Sooner, full stop. The "one more year" years ([[one-more-year]]) are lived, in hindsight, as the real cost of the project: years of full health traded for percentage points the plan never needed. The corpus's second regret is the first one's cousin: "I lived too thin in the early years", the underspending again. The pattern fits what the psychology of regret finds in the general population: looking back, people regret their inactions far more than their actions (Gilovich and Medvec, 1995). The **opposite** regrets exist, from people who left too tight and had to go back to work, but they are a clear minority here. Survivorship bias being what it is, the real lesson is conditional. With a plan built to this book's standards (margins named, pension counted, rule written), the statistically dominant mistake is too much caution, not too little.
:::

## The canonical list: the advice the veterans repeat

Compiled from the "advice for candidates" threads, here are the ten that always come back, each with its chapter:

1. **Prototype the life before you leave** (sabbatical, part-time): the most frequent advice of all ([[the-three-phases]]).
2. **Leave toward something**, never just away from a job you hate ([[meaning-and-identity]]).
3. **Treat year one as convalescence**: no big decisions, no move, no giant project.
4. **Your social life is a line in the plan**: invest in it deliberately.
5. **Expect to make money without looking for it**, but do not count it in advance ([[how-much-you-need]]).
6. **Automate the paycheck, space out the statements** ([[the-psychology-of-spending]]).
7. **Flexibility is easier to promise than to live**: put a number on the floor **first** ([[flexibility-in-practice]]).
8. **Sync up as a couple**: on the budget and on the life ([[couples-and-family]]).
9. **Do not argue your FIRE at the office or at dinner parties**: nobody gets it, that is the norm, and the dedicated communities exist for exactly that.
10. **The meta-advice**: "the financial part was the easy part". It comes from people who spent years on that financial part, so do not misread it. It is **necessary**, it can be mastered, and once it is mastered the real work starts.

## The corpus, and where to read it

If you want to go further, here are the sources that last. Start with the long-form diaries. LivingAFI's diary is the most honest post-FIRE account on the web, the return to work included. Early Retirement Now's anniversary reviews, published since 2018, give the quantitative version ([[the-ern-series]]). Then the oldest veterans, Mr. Money Mustache and his generation; read their **recent** posts, not the founding ones. Then the forums with long threads: [r/financialindependence](https://www.reddit.com/r/financialindependence) and its year-end "annual updates", and the Mr. Money Mustache forum with its post-FIRE section. Wherever you live, the local rules are the one thing no foreign account can settle for you ([[us-healthcare-and-social-security]]). Last, the qualitative studies, still in their infancy; academic research is starting to interview FIRE cohorts, worth watching. The reading filter stays the same: written as it happened rather than in hindsight, numbers rather than narrative, failures actively sought out.

## The essentials

- Among people who left with a real plan, the financial machinery works and income keeps arriving without being chased: in the real accounts, the crises are almost never financial. They are crises of identity, of structure, of connection.
- The time pattern: honeymoon (6 to 12 months), then the eighteen-month wall. You leave toward something, prototyped beforehand, and year one is treated as convalescence.
- Your social life and your couple are lines in the plan: work supplied one, the exit tests the other. Deliberate investment required.
- Financial anxiety fades through the **test**, the first drop lived through, not through the spreadsheet: equip the early years and let experience do the rest.
- The veterans' number one regret, unanimously: "I should have left sooner" (and lived larger in the early years). With a plan built to this book's standards, the dominant mistake is too much caution. Read [[one-more-year]] again before you push the date back once more.
