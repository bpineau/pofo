# FIRE as a couple, and as a family
<!-- source: couple-et-famille @ 761fe13275b2 -->

Simulators plan for individuals. Life hands those plans to families. The corpus of accounts, the wins and the wrecks alike, is blunt about it: **FIRE is a team sport**. The biggest financial risk to a plan is not the sequence, and it is not inflation. It is divorce. It cuts the capital in half and nearly doubles the costs, which no crash does. And what the happy stories have in common is a project genuinely **shared**, not one half endured by the other.

This chapter follows the family side of the plan, phase by phase. The couple during accumulation, where a mismatch in appetite is the movement's most common slow-burning conflict. The exit, together or staggered, an underrated call. Life together outside work, the close quarters and the territories. The household's financial **governance**, where the single-manager problem hides: half the plans in this book could not be run by the surviving spouse, and that is a design flaw exactly like a bad rate. Divorce, preventing it and looking straight at it. Children, their datable cost, the example you set, the help you give them as adults. And aging parents, the sandwich generation that FIRE, with time on its hands, joins more often than its share.

::: cle The two rules of a team sport
First, the plan belongs to **both of you**. A FIRE imposed on someone does not hold. A spouse dragged into a frugality they never chose is a debt, and it gets paid back with interest: friction at best, a breakup at worst. And a breakup is ruin. The pace of the less eager one is the pace of the project.

Second, the plan has to be **runnable by either of you**. If only one of you understands the accounts, the withdrawal rule and the thresholds, the plan has a single point of failure. The written page, the letter of instructions and an annual review done **together** are design requirements, not courtesies ([[building-your-plan]]).
:::

## Accumulating together: the mismatch in appetite

One founding conflict shows up in most couples in the movement. One of them finds FIRE, buys in, starts optimizing. The other likes their job, or their comfort, or simply has a different relationship with security and time. The project then turns into a quiet standoff, made of complaints about spending, spreadsheets waved around, and "you don't get it".

The couples who last tell a different story. They built the project on shared **life goals** before talking about the means. Time, freedom, less stress: agreement comes easily there. The savings rate is a harder negotiation, so it comes second. Each keeps a **personal budget** to spend without justifying it, because peace is worth its carrying cost. They spell out the **trade-offs** instead of tallying frustrations, since a happy FIRE at 50, together, beats a bitter one at 46. And nobody gets converted by force. You share the data ([[what-is-fire]], this book), you let it sit, and you accept that the other picks Coast when you were dreaming of Lean ([[how-much-you-need]]). The budget for the life you are **aiming at** gets negotiated by two. It does not get decreed by one.

## The exit: together or staggered?

This is the least discussed call and one of the most structural. The **staggered** exit, where one leaves while the other keeps working, has huge financial virtues. The remaining salary covers all or part of the spending through the fragile window, and that is the best insurance against a bad sequence in this book ([[sequence-of-returns]], [[pensions-and-other-income]]). The employer health coverage stays in place, and the retirement credits keep accruing ([[us-healthcare-and-social-security]]).

It has real human costs too. The two lives fall out of step: one is free, the other is at the office, vacations become impossible, and a mild jealousy settles in. The free one slides fast into the role of household manager, the number one source of friction in staggered exits. The **simultaneous** exit flips the picture. You gain the shared adventure and the projects done together. You lose the salary safety net, and the close quarters arrive all at once.

The happy accounts almost always land on a **short, dated** gap of 12 to 36 months, chosen together, with a firm exit date for the second one. A gap that is endured and open-ended is the worst setup of all. Through that window, set the rules of close quarters in advance (below) and split the housework **explicitly**. The free one does not become the default homemaker. That gets decided.

## Together 24 hours a day

Simultaneous exits produce a quiet shock. Couples who saw each other three hours a day are suddenly together nonstop. They find out that love is not the same skill as living in close quarters.

The tools are mundane, and they come up in every account. Draw **territories**: your own rooms, your own time slots, your own activities, and negotiate the shape of an ordinary day the way friendly roommates would. Keep **separate social lives** alongside the shared one, because a spouse cannot be the whole infrastructure ([[meaning-and-identity]]). Run **separate projects** alongside the joint ones, because boredom in pairs weighs more than boredom alone. One calendar note is left. The first year, when **both** of you go through convalescence at the same time, is the delicate one ([[voices-from-real-retirees]]). Knowing it in advance defuses half the scares: it is not us, it is the phase.

::: attention Governance: the single-manager problem
In most households in the movement, one of the two built the plan: accounts, allocation, rule, thresholds. The other trusts them. It is a comfortable setup, and a **dangerous** one. If the manager dies or is disabled, the other inherits a machine they cannot fly, at the worst possible moment, surrounded by delighted salespeople.

Four minimum requirements, none of them optional. A **one-page plan** the other can read, with the rule, the amounts, the thresholds and the never-dos ([[building-your-plan]]). A **letter of instructions** listing accounts, access and contacts. An **annual review done together**, two hours a year, at the end of which the non-manager can say "I know where we stand, and what to do if": that is what the review is really teaching ([[the-annual-review]]). And a plan **deliberately** simplified to the level of its weakest executor. A bounded Vanguard-style rule both of you can apply beats an optimal ABW machine only one of you can run ([[choosing-your-strategy]]). Governance is a first-order criterion, and here is what it looks like in the flesh.

Test yourselves. If your spouse had to take over on Monday, what would happen? If the answer is "they would call our banker", the plan has a hole.
:::

## Divorce: looking straight at it

Nobody plans a divorce, and a book about managing risk has no right to leave it out. This is the scenario that cuts the capital in half and inflates the needs at the same time, with two homes and two lives. Financially, no crash in history hurts this much, because that capital never comes back.

What belongs in this book comes down to three things. **Prevention** is not financial: it is this whole chapter. A shared project, explicit trade-offs, a life together with the right tools. FIRE run badly is a documented risk factor for a marriage, frugality imposed and close quarters unprepared at the top of the list. The **framework** gets chosen while things are calm. Depending on where you live, the marital-property rules, a prenuptial agreement or a cohabitation agreement decide who owns what and what passes to whom; unmarried partners are usually the least protected of all, and in some places they inherit almost nothing by default. Whatever they are called locally, these are tools of plain fairness, worth an hour with a lawyer **before** you need them, and clarity about who owns what protects a couple far more than it threatens it. And **if it happens**, you rebuild the plan. You re-run both halves, often two Coast FIRE plans where there had been one FIRE. Flexibility, a partial return to work and the years that follow do the rest, and the post-divorce accounts in the corpus are hard but rarely final wrecks. What cannot be rebuilt is having bet everything on a plan that assumed the couple was forever, without ever saying so.

## Children, and parents

**Children in the plan** are three separate subjects.

Their **cost** can be dated and funded. Higher education runs EUR 10k to EUR 25k a year for 3 to 5 years per child, on dates you know fifteen years ahead: the textbook case for a bond ladder ([[bond-ladders]], [[how-much-you-need]]).

Then the **example**. Growing up with parents who are free, present and frugal **by choice** is, in the accounts of FIRE parents, the project's number one benefit: "the real inheritance is the model". As long as you explain it. Children of an unexplained FIRE sometimes conclude "we are poor" or "money is not to be talked about". Teaching them how money works is a workstream of the project in its own right.

Last, **helping adult children**. The best moment to pass money on is often when they are setting up, between 25 and 35: the down payment, the venture, the moment when EUR 30k changes a trajectory. Gifts made while you are alive stay under the golden rule, because they come out of the surplus, never out of the plan's margins.

**Aging parents** are the subject FIRE runs into more often than most. Free time makes you the sibling designated as the **natural caregiver**. It is a gift: time given and simple presence rank among the finest entries in the corpus when people take stock. It is also a risk worth naming. Care can turn into a full-time job you never chose. It has a cost, since in some places the law can send a parent's long-term-care bill to the children, so a plan that provisions for your own care should look upstream as well. And it raises a fairness question, because whoever gives their time should not also be the only one paying. Have those conversations with your siblings early. The plan can carry a "family support" line: a named reserve beats a recurring surprise.

::: exemple The annual review for two, in real life
At Nadia and Marc's, the January review runs two hours and follows a well-worn order.

1) Marc, the manager, walks through the plan page. Current rate 3.7%, every light green, rebalancing done by the withdrawals. Ten minutes.

2) Then the reverse quiz. Nadia explains to **Marc** what she would do if he were hospitalized tomorrow: the letter of instructions, the monthly transfer, "we sell nothing, we call no one". Fifteen minutes, and that is the real deliverable.

3) The budget, together. The travel line is underspent again, and the call is made: booking **required** before March ([[the-psychology-of-spending]]).

4) The family line. Léo starts college in 4 years and the matching rungs of the ladder are funded. Marc's father is declining, so a conversation with his siblings goes on the calendar.

5) The non-financial line, each person's four workstreams ([[meaning-and-identity]]). Nadia is doing well; Marc is bored, and out comes his list of hypotheses again.

Two hours a year, and the plan, the couple and the family stay inside the same loop. That is all the governance a good plan asks for.
:::

## The essentials

- FIRE is a team sport. Divorce is the only "crash" that cuts the capital in half and nearly doubles the costs, and preventing it is not a financial job: a shared project run at the pace of the less eager one, explicit trade-offs, personal budgets nobody has to justify.
- The staggered exit is the best insurance against a bad sequence there is, through the salary, the health coverage and the retirement credits, at the price of two lives falling out of step. The good version is **short and dated**, with the housework decided rather than defaulted.
- Governance is a design requirement: a one-page plan, a letter of instructions, an annual review done **together** with a reverse quiz, and a rule sized for its weakest executor. A single manager is a single point of failure.
- Children have a cost you can date and fund with a ladder. The real inheritance is the example, as long as you explain it, or it gets read the wrong way. And you help them at the moment the help changes a trajectory. With parents, time given is a gift, but the help gets bounded and shared among siblings, early.
- The marital-property rules, whatever they are called where you live, and the paperwork behind them get chosen while things are calm, because clarity about who owns what protects a couple. And a plan that assumes the couple is forever without ever saying so is not a plan.
