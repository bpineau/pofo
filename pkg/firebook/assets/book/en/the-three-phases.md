# Accumulation, transition, withdrawal: the three lives of a FIRE plan
<!-- source: les-trois-phases @ 48cf1a0d7cee -->

A FIRE plan is not a state, it is a path. It runs through three phases, and they do not obey the same rules. Accumulation: you save, and time works for you. Transition: the five years around the exit, the most dangerous stretch of the whole journey. Withdrawal: you live off the capital, and you steer. Most of the expensive mistakes come from one move, using the reflexes of one phase in another. The aggression of accumulation during the transition. The caution of withdrawal during accumulation.

This page lays out what changes in each phase, what to optimize there, and the moves that carry you from one to the next. It is the book's spine: nearly every other article hangs off one of the three.

::: cle What really changes between the phases
One variable governs everything: the **direction** and the **size** of the flows between you and the portfolio. While you accumulate you pay in, and crashes make you richer, because you buy low. While you withdraw you draw on the portfolio, and crashes bleed you, because you sell low ([[sequence-of-returns]]). At the transition the flows reverse. That is also, mechanically, where the portfolio is at its largest against the flows. Every percent of decline costs the most dollars there, and mistakes are the hardest to undo. Same portfolio, same market: three different problems.
:::

::: figure flux-relatif-phases
A deterministic projection on this article's own assumptions (34% saved, 5% then 4% real return, exit at 45), not a simulation. The year's flow, measured against the portfolio it lands in, shrinks for twenty-five years, hits its low point on the day you quit, and changes sign.
:::

## Phase 1: accumulation, the decade (or two) when everything is simple

The good news about accumulation is that it is the best understood phase, and that almost everything that complicates it is noise. The decisions, in order of how much they actually matter:

**1. The savings rate dominates everything** ([[what-is-fire]]). Going from 20% to 35% saved pulls independence in by more than ten years, and no portfolio decision comes close to that. The wins come from the three big lines (housing, transportation, food), not from your subscriptions.

**2. Automation beats discipline.** An automatic transfer on payday, a standing investment order, and the budget lives on what is left. Willpower runs out; the way you wire your money spends none of it.

**3. The portfolio can be aggressive, because the flows protect you.** 80 to 100% in global equities defends itself as long as the first withdrawal is more than 10 to 15 years away. An early crash is a buyer's windfall, and the order of returns never works against you while there are no withdrawals ([[arithmetic-vs-geometric-returns]]). The classic mistake here is not aggression, it is infidelity: switching strategy with the fashions, selling into declines, collecting funds.

**4. The accounts get set up now.** A tax shelter mostly pays you in years, and years cannot be bought back later: some clocks only start once an account is open and funded, and contribution room accrues year by year and is gone if you skip it. Opening the right accounts early, even with a token amount, and filling them in the right order is one of the few genuinely free things in this business ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]]). The 45-year-old living off a portfolio inherits the choices the 30-year-old made.

**5. Track little, but track it well.** One check a year is enough: the savings rate you actually hit, invested wealth, distance to target ([[how-much-you-need]]). Fine-grained steering is a phase 3 tool. In phase 1 it mostly manufactures anxiety and trades.

::: astuce The two-number rule
Keep two numbers current, not twenty: your multiple today (invested wealth divided by annual spending) and your savings rate over the trailing twelve months. The first says where you stand (independence sits between 25 and 33, [[how-much-you-need]]), the second how fast you are getting there. Every other line of personal reporting is decoration.
:::

## Phase 2: the transition, the five years that decide everything

The red zone of the plan runs from two years before you hit your target to three years after you quit. The portfolio has never been this large against the flows that could repair it. The fragile window of sequence risk opens ([[sequence-of-returns]]). And irreversible decisions get made: leaving a job, a title, a level of income. What research and practice say to do there:

**De-risk the portfolio, temporarily.** This is where Kitces's bond tent gets built, the temporary hump around the exit. You cut equity exposure as the date approaches, down toward 50 to 60%. Then you take it back up, gradually, once the fragile window is behind you ([[glidepaths]]). Permanent caution costs too much return. Caution concentrated on the dangerous years captures most of the protection for a fraction of the cost. This is also when you build the cash buffer ([[cash-buffer]]): two to three years of spending, set aside in cash or a money-market fund out of your last paychecks, rather than raised by selling stocks on the way out.

**Switch the accounting into withdrawal mode.** The three-tier budget (floor, comfort, dream), an estimate of the tax friction, your future pensions documented: the whole apparatus of [[how-much-you-need]] gets built and tested now, against your real spending over the last two years.

**Prototype the life that comes next.** A sabbatical, part-time work, three months genuinely unplugged: testing the life you are aiming at before you hand back the badge heads off the most common failure of all, the emptiness ([[meaning-and-identity]], [[the-psychology-of-spending]]). It is also when the serious conversations with your partner happen ([[couples-and-family]]).

**Write the operating plan.** The withdrawal strategy you picked and its thresholds ([[choosing-your-strategy]]), the conditions for leaving, your answers to bad years written before you need them ([[when-to-worry]]): all of it on paper, while you are calm. A plan written in a bull market is worth infinitely more than improvisation in a crash.

**Handle the asymmetry of career risk.** The last point, and the one most often skipped. In this phase one more year of salary is worth a lot. It fills the buffer, pushes the fragile window back, and adds to the pension you will eventually claim. But each repeat year is worth less than the one before ([[one-more-year]]). A well-run transition has written exit criteria; without them it lasts forever.

::: attention The trap at the top
Odds are you reach your target during a bull market, since a bull market is what carries you there, which means high valuations ([[valuations-and-cape]]), which means exactly the conditions history ties to the worst retirement vintages ([[the-trinity-study]]). "I hit my number" and "this is a good time to leave" are two different statements. The defenses: an initial rate set against valuations ([[cape-based-rules]]), the bond tent, a full buffer, and some income in the first year or two ([[going-back-to-work]]).
:::

## Phase 3: withdrawal, the art of steering

The day of your first withdrawal you change jobs: from saver (pile it up, ignore the noise) to income manager (deliver a stable income out of an unstable asset). The rules of the new job:

**Execute a strategy, not a mood.** The withdrawal rule you chose in phase 2 ([[withdrawal-strategies-overview]]) runs mechanically: amounts, dates, adjustment thresholds. Improvisation is almost always procyclical. You spend freely at the top and panic at the bottom. The reverse would be no better: spending freely in a trough eats capital at the worst possible moment. What protects you is a rule that makes the call for you, in both directions and in small steps.

**Cruising speed: one real check a year.** The annual review ([[the-annual-review]]) recomputes the current withdrawal rate, tests the thresholds, rebalances the portfolio, refills or draws on the buffer per the rules ([[refilling-the-buffer]]), and updates pensions and taxes. Between two reviews: nothing, unless a written threshold is breached. Looking every day improves no decision and worsens every emotion.

**Steer by the warning lights, not the balance.** A portfolio balance either scares you or intoxicates you. The useful instruments are ratios: the current withdrawal rate (spending divided by portfolio, the main warning light), the distance to your floor, the buffer years left. Typical thresholds: under 4.5% and all is well; 4.5 to 5.5% and you watch it and trim a little; above that, the adjustment rule applies ([[when-to-worry]]).

**Do not forget to spend.** Ruin has a mirror image, and it is the likelier one among people who were good at saving: dying on a pile of gold after decades of self-denial in the name of caution ([[the-psychology-of-spending]], [[spending-in-retirement]]). Floor-and-ceiling rules exist to authorize the good years, not only to cut the bad ones.

**Let the phase evolve.** Withdrawal is not one flat stretch. The fragile window of the early years demands the most attention. Ten years in with no major accident, the game is statistically won and the steering gets lighter. When the pensions start ([[pensions-and-other-income]]) the picture changes again, often enough to close the question of ruin for good.

::: exemple The same person, three jobs
Inès at 36, saving 34%, 90% equities: her job is to automate and not look. Inès at 44, at 90% of her target. She takes equities down to 55%, moves 30 months of spending into a money-market fund, tries a four-day week, writes her withdrawal rule and sets her criteria for leaving. Inès at 47, two years out, in a mediocre market: current rate 4.8%, amber. She applies her rule (comfort minus 8%), draws on the buffer instead of selling stocks, and touches nothing else. Three snapshots, three jobs, one plan.
:::

## The essentials

- Three phases, three jobs: accumulate (the savings rate dominates, aggression defends itself), transition (de-risk temporarily, write everything down, prototype the life), withdraw (execute, steer by ratios, one check a year).
- The transition is the red zone: the portfolio at its largest against the flows, the fragile window ahead, irreversible decisions. Plans are won and lost there.
- The structural trap: you reach your target at the top, so at high valuations. The defenses are in [[glidepaths]], [[cash-buffer]] and [[cape-based-rules]].
- Each phase's reflexes are toxic in another: aggression in the transition, permanent caution in accumulation, improvisation in withdrawal.
- Nearly every article in this book equips one of the three phases. Read [[sequence-of-returns]] next, then [[building-your-plan]].
