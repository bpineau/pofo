# When to worry, and when to let it ride
<!-- source: quand-s-inquieter @ e69a930d196f -->

Between two annual reviews, a retiree lives with one nagging question. "With the market doing this, right now, should I be doing something?" This book's answer is almost always no. But that no needs **instruments** behind it to hold up. Doing nothing with no instruments is just holding your breath. Doing nothing while watching your warning lights is flying the plan ([[the-psychology-of-spending]]: numbers are what put bounds on fear).

This chapter builds the dashboard you fly between reviews. One warning light does most of the work: the current withdrawal rate, on a green-amber-red scale calibrated to your own plan. Next comes the list of everything that is **not** a signal, because sorting noise from signal is half the job. Then the early warnings that are real. They exist, they are slow, and their slowness is what saves you: ruin gives 8 to 15 years of notice ([[failure-probability]]). Last, the ladder of graded responses. Worrying usefully means knowing in advance what you will do at each step, from doing nothing on purpose to redesigning the plan from scratch. It is the operating version of ERN's Part 47, "When to Worry, When to Wing It" ([[the-ern-series]]).

::: cle The principle behind the dashboard
You steer by **ratios**, never by balances. A balance scares you or elates you without telling you anything, because it only means something next to your spending and your horizon. The **current withdrawal rate** says it all in one number: annual spending divided by today's portfolio. It tells you where the plan stands, whatever moved it, the market, your spending or inflation. Anyone can work it out in their head ([[couples-and-family]]: it is the number the reverse quiz turns on), and its thresholds can be calibrated to you. That is the main warning light. Everything else is a supplement.
:::

## The main warning light: the current rate and its scale

**Why this one?** The current rate folds all three threats into a single number. A falling market, spending that drifts, inflation inflating the withdrawal: every one of them pushes it up ([[inflation-and-withdrawal-rates]]). It is also the input the guardrail rules read ([[guyton-klinger]]). And it has a robust absolute reading. Sit above roughly 5.5 to 6% for long and few plans still in their bridge years survive without an adjustment. Sit under 3.5% for long and almost all of them thrive ([[the-4-percent-rule]] does the arithmetic of what is sustainable). Its limit is well known: it ignores the horizon left and the pensions still to come. That is why risk-based guardrails replace it at the annual review, with a fresh simulation ([[morningstar-guardrails]]). Between reviews, though, simplicity wins. This dashboard does not need a simulator. It needs one number you can read on a Sunday night in October 2008.

**The scale, calibrated to your plan.** The values below belong to a bridge-phase plan that started at 3.6%. Yours come out of a simulator ([[building-your-plan]], block 5 of the template).

- **Green** (under about 4.3%, up to roughly 20% above the starting rate): normal operation. Every ordinary market decline lives here, and the response is one word, nothing. Literally nothing, because the plan's fan already contained this path ([[reading-a-fan-chart]]).
- **Amber** (about 4.3 to 5.2%): the watch zone. You act gently: painless savings, an indexation freeze if the rule provides for one, and the buffer doing its job at its own thresholds ([[refilling-the-buffer]]). The watch tightens too, monthly instead of quarterly. No cut, and nothing changes in the portfolio.
- **Red** (above about 5.2%, confirmed on two readings spaced apart, the hysteresis that filters false alarms): the action zone. The written cut takes effect ([[flexibility-in-practice]]), side income switches on if the plan provided for it ([[going-back-to-work]]), and the review is pulled forward. This is the only case where the calendar gives way.

## Sorting: what is not a signal

Half of flying a plan is filtering, and this list earns its place in the plan itself (the never-dos block, [[building-your-plan]]). Headlines are not a signal. Apocalyptic front pages are an audience product, not plan information, and no forty-year decision gets made on a twenty-four-hour news cycle. Neither is the year's performance. One year, even at −20%, is noise the plan already simulated. The question is never "did the market fall?" but "did my warning light change color?". Predictions count for no more, including the strategist who "sees" the crash or the rebound coming, because market timing was settled earlier in this book ([[simulator-traps]], [[valuations-and-cape]]). Other people's portfolios are not a signal either, not the neighbor in crypto, not the cousin in rental property, because your plan answers a spec that no dinner-party story shares ([[the-psychology-of-spending]]). And the bumps of ordinary life, the expensive month or the spendy quarter, do not count: spending is judged on a rolling annual average, not on a statement. Information hygiene follows the same logic. You look at the portfolio at the quarterly check, monthly in amber. Financial news is consumed like sugar: sparingly, and never on the evening of a decline.

## The real signals: slow, cumulative, and thank goodness

A plan that is genuinely going wrong does not look like a crash. It looks like a drift. The historical paths that failed all share one profile ([[failure-probability]]). Year after year of a current rate that is high and still climbing, with the squeeze settling in: spending climbs with inflation while the portfolio stalls ([[fixed-inflation-adjusted-withdrawal]]). Amber that never goes back to green, then red that stays. It takes 8 to 15 years, it is visible at every review, and there is time to run three steps of response before anything turns irreversible.

The other signals are slow too. Real spending can settle structurally above the plan. Three reviews running and it is no longer a gap, it is the new normal, and the plan has to absorb it or treat it. Personal inflation can diverge from the index for years ([[tracking-inflation]], where health costs run away). Then there are the life events, health, family, divorce, the shocks that really hit a plan ([[couples-and-family]]). Those justify an immediate off-cycle review. The calendar protects you from the market's moods, not from the facts of your life.

::: science The ladder of graded responses: the full playbook
The ladder is written into the plan and it is climbed in **order**. Each step gets 6 to 12 months before you move to the next, except on a confirmed red.

- **Step 0, nothing**: normal operation in green, 90% of years.
- **Step 1, the painless moves**: in amber, gentle savings, big discretionary purchases pushed back, an indexation freeze, about 5 to 8% off the spending without touching your life ([[flexibility-in-practice]]).
- **Step 2, the written cut**: on a confirmed red, the rule's −10 to −15%, never below the floor, with the buffer switching on at its own thresholds, the two working together ([[refilling-the-buffer]]).
- **Step 3, income**: on a red that persists past 18 to 24 months, you exercise the option you kept alive, and $8k to $15k a year of contract work bends the curve more than any cut ([[going-back-to-work]]). This step is what the upkeep premium bought.
- **Step 4, the redesign**: a red that comes through steps 2 and 3 without bending. Rare. A full redesign, made while calm: the rate recomputed, the big assets on the table ([[real-estate-in-retirement]]), claiming a pension earlier put under study. This is plan v2, painful, orderly, and always available.

That is what the slowness of ruin really buys: time to rebuild. The playbook fits in five lines of the template. Just having it in writing defuses most of the anxiety. What you truly dread is whatever you have no plan for.
:::

::: exemple 2033 to 2036 on the dashboard
Back to Aline and Thomas in their bad stretch ([[the-annual-review]]). In mid-2033 the market drops 19% and $310k melts off their balance. Their Sunday-night question is not "what do we do?" but "what color are we?".

- **Mid-2033**: current rate 4.6%, amber. Step 1: the indexation freeze already provided for, the spring trip pushed back, the monthly check switched on. That is all. Not one holding moves.
- **Early 2034**: another leg down takes the rate to 5.4%, so red, but that is only the first reading, and the hysteresis demands confirmation.
- **Three months later**: 5.3%, red confirmed. The written cut applies, step 2, −10%, down to $48,600, and the buffer carries the withdrawals. The decision took ten minutes. It had been written since 2029.
- **Mid-2035**: recovery to 4.4%, then 3.9%. The steps are climbed back down in reverse order: the cut lifted once the rate is under the threshold again, indexation restarted, the buffer refilled in calm weather.

The tally of the crossing, the years spent inside a hostile market, as written in the log: two and a half years, two steps, zero sleepless nights over a decision, zero panic selling. And Thomas's line at the 2036 review sums up the chapter: "we never had to be brave, we only had to follow the list".
:::

## The essentials

- You steer by ratios, not balances. The current withdrawal rate is the warning light you fly between reviews: simple, doable in your head, folding market, spending and inflation into one number, on a green-amber-red scale calibrated to your plan, with hysteresis and confirmation before any red.
- Sorting noise from signal is half the job. Headlines, the year's performance, predictions and the neighbors' portfolios are never signals. Spending is judged on an annual average, and information hygiene gets written into the plan.
- The real signals are slow and cumulative: amber that never returns to green, spending structurally above plan, personal inflation drifting away from the index. Ruin gives 5 to 15 years of notice, and that slowness is your margin. Life events, on the other hand, trigger an immediate review.
- The five-step playbook (nothing, then the painless moves, then the written cut plus the buffer, then income, then the redesign) is climbed in order and climbed back down in reverse. Having it in writing defuses anxiety better than any number.
- Flying a plan well looks like boredom: years of step 0, a color checked now and then, a list followed. "We never had to be brave" is the verdict on a well-designed plan.
