# Building it with US-listed ETFs: the retiree's shopping list
<!-- source: etf-ucits-europeens @ 0f0f1b13620c -->

The withdrawal literature is written in dollars, and the plumbing it assumes is already sitting in your brokerage account. Every block in this part of the book has a US-listed fund behind it, and most of them cost between three and twenty-five basis points a year. Access is not the problem. The problem is that the American fund industry will sell you an expensive version of a cheap idea in every aisle, and that the tax code cares a great deal about which account each block goes in.

This article is the assembly guide. It covers what an ETF really is, and the four technical choices that matter when you pick one. It sets out the full cost chain and the one number that measures it, the tracking difference rather than the expense ratio. It gives the table of blocks, one line per article of this part, with the best-known tickers in each category. And it closes on the practice of selling, because turning a portfolio into a paycheck every month is a skill of its own.

::: cle Your paycheck is a sell order
An American fund must pass its income through to you: the accumulating share class European investors use does not exist here, and you cannot switch a distribution off. So the reversal still has to happen, and you make it with sell orders instead of share classes. The habit is to look for "income": dividend funds, high yield, covered calls. In the withdrawal phase you want the opposite. Hold the broad, cheap fund, let its dividend land where it lands, and manufacture your income by **selling** the exact amount your rule calls for. The gain is threefold. Control first: **your** rule sets the amount, not a payout policy ([[withdrawal-strategies-overview]]). Then taxes: a sale is taxed only on its **gain** component, and you choose which shares to sell, while a dividend is taxed on the whole check with no such choice. Last, you dodge the yield trap, because chasing payout quietly deforms the portfolio ([[defensive-assets]]). A modern retiree's income is a monthly sell order, not a coupon.
:::

## What a US ETF is, and the four technical choices

**The wrapper.** Nearly every fund in the table below is an open-end investment company registered with the SEC under the Investment Company Act of 1940. That is what buys the structural protections: assets held by an independent custodian, daily pricing, diversification rules, hard limits on leverage and on affiliated transactions, a board that answers for the fund. The risk in a broad ETF is the risk of its **market**, not the risk of its sponsor. What the wrapper does not guarantee is the quality of the index, the level of the fee, or that the product makes any sense. There are absurd 1940 Act funds.

The mechanism worth understanding is creation and redemption in kind. Authorized participants swap baskets of the underlying securities for blocks of ETF shares, which lets the fund push out its lowest-basis shares to meet redemptions instead of selling them. A broad equity index ETF therefore distributes essentially no capital gains, while an index mutual fund holding exactly the same stocks can hand you a taxable gain in a year when you did nothing at all. In a taxable account that difference is worth more than most of the fee arguments.

**Choice 1: ETF or mutual fund.** In a taxable account, take the ETF, for the reason just given. Inside an IRA or a 401(k) it barely matters, and the plan menu usually decides for you. One footnote: Vanguard's index mutual funds have long carried an ETF share class of the same portfolio, which makes them as tax-efficient as the ETF, and since 2025 the SEC has been granting other sponsors the same relief, so the gap closes fund by fund.

**Choice 2: the distribution is not optional.** Your total-market fund pays a dividend every quarter whether you asked for it or not, and in a taxable account it is taxed the year it arrives: qualified dividends at the long-term capital gain rate, bond and money-market income at your ordinary rate. Two consequences follow. Put the high-income blocks where their income is invisible ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]]), and treat the cash that lands in the taxable account as the first source of your next withdrawal, before you sell anything.

**Choice 3: physical replication, and the three blocks that are not funds.** US index ETFs hold the securities. Synthetic swap-based replication, the European workhorse, is rare here and you can ignore it. But three blocks in the table are not 1940 Act funds at all, and that changes their taxes. A physically backed gold fund is a grantor trust, so a long-term gain on it is taxed as a **collectible**, at a maximum rate of 28% instead of 20% (IRS Topic 409). Some commodity and futures products are partnerships that mail a Schedule K-1 in March instead of a 1099. The managed-futures ETFs below avoid the K-1 by routing their futures through a Cayman subsidiary, but they distribute ordinary income, and lumpy amounts of it. None of this disqualifies a block. It decides which account the block belongs in.

**Choice 4: the sponsor, the fee, and what the fund lends.** Prefer large, old, cheap funds from a sponsor that competes on price. Past roughly $1B in assets and a few years of history, a closure is not a live worry. Then read one line of the annual report: securities lending. Most equity ETFs lend their shares out, the borrower posts collateral, and the fund keeps the bulk of the revenue, which is why a well-run fund sometimes beats its index before fees. It is a small, collateralized, real risk taken on your behalf, and you want to know how the revenue is split.

One thing you can stop worrying about is domicile. The Irish-domicile arbitrage a European investor works so hard for exists to cut the US withholding tax on a foreign fund. You are the domestic investor and there is nothing to reclaim. The one echo of it worth knowing: a fund with most of its assets abroad passes the foreign tax it paid through to you as a credit, and that credit is only usable if the fund sits in a taxable account. What you choose in place of domicile is the **account**, and that choice is worth more than every fee difference in the table below ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]]).

## The costs: the whole chain, and the real measure

The expense ratio on the label, 0.03% to 0.90% across the blocks of this book, is only the first link. The chain has five. The expense ratio first. Then the fund's own internal drift, from sampling, from trading and from lending revenue, which runs either way and is sometimes a **gain**. Then the bid-ask spread you pay going in and coming out, a penny on the largest equity funds and more like 0.05 to 0.20% on a thin managed-futures fund. Then commissions, now zero on listed ETFs at every large US broker. And last the layer that dominates everything when it exists: about 1% a year of advisory fee on assets, or the expense load buried in a variable annuity subaccount.

The number that aggregates the first two links is the **tracking difference**: the fund's total return minus its index's total return, over three to five years, published by the sponsor and by the screeners. That is what you compare between two candidates, not the expense ratio. A complete portfolio built out of the table costs **0.10 to 0.25% a year all in**, against 1.5 to 2% for the same allocation delivered through an advisor in loaded funds. Over a 40-year retirement, that gap is worth something like half a point to a full point of withdrawal rate. The plumbing is worth as much as most of the strategy debates ([[expected-returns]]).

The assembly rule fits in one sentence: **six to eight holdings** cover the whole program of this part. Past that, every extra line has to evict an existing one or fill a role the table does not cover. Fund collecting, fifteen overlapping positions, is the most common cosmetic mistake there is. It adds no diversification, because the indexes overlap heavily, and it complicates every rebalance, every tax lot and every estate ([[couples-and-family]]).

::: astuce Where each block goes, in one rule
The full account mechanics are in [[us-accounts-and-account-order]]. The placement rule that comes out of them takes three lines. The **tax-deferred** accounts, traditional 401(k) and traditional IRA, take everything that throws off ordinary income: the bond core, TIPS, gold with its collectibles rate, managed futures with their lumpy distributions. The **Roth** takes the block you expect to grow the most and hold the longest, which is stocks, because nothing that happens inside it is ever taxed. The **taxable** brokerage takes the broad equity funds: they distribute little, mostly as qualified dividends, they carry the foreign tax credit on their international leg, they give you lot-level control over every sale, and whatever you never sell gets a step-up in basis at death. One caveat keeps this honest. Placement is a second-order optimization worth a few tenths of a point a year. It never justifies holding the wrong allocation because the right one would not fit the account.
:::

## Selling cleanly

When the time comes, the portfolio gets consumed. A few habits keep the friction down.

**The scheduled sale.** It funds the monthly or quarterly paycheck ([[fixed-inflation-adjusted-withdrawal]]). Sell the line the rebalancing draw points at, the overweight one. Trade in the middle of the session and not in the first or last fifteen minutes, when spreads are widest and some of the fund's holdings may not be trading yet. Never a market order on a thin fund: a limit order at the midpoint costs one more click and saves basis points. Settlement has been T+1 since May 2024, so cash from a Tuesday sale is there on Wednesday. Leave a couple of days of slack ahead of a transfer date rather than trading on the morning the money is due.

**Which shares you sell.** Here the American retiree holds a lever the European does not. Your broker's default is usually first in, first out, which reaches for your oldest and lowest-basis shares. Switch the account to **specific identification**, and pick the lots before or at the moment of the trade, because the IRS wants the identification made then and not reconstructed in April (Publication 550). Every withdrawal then becomes a choice: sell high-basis lots to keep the realized gain small in a year when you are managing your taxable income, or sell low-basis lots on purpose in a low-income year to reset your basis higher. Average basis is available only for mutual fund shares, and the election is sticky, which is one more reason to keep the taxable account in ETFs. On the holding period, more than one year gets the long-term rate: 0, 15 or 20% depending on taxable income, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax higher up. Before pensions and Social Security start, the 0% band is often wide open, and withdrawals taken inside it can cost almost nothing ([[us-taxes-in-the-withdrawal-phase]]).

**Losses, and the 30-day window.** In a bad year, the sale that funds your spending will sometimes realize a loss. Take it: it offsets your gains, then $3,000 of ordinary income a year, and the rest carries forward. But do not buy the same fund back within 30 days before or after the sale, in any account you control, your spouse's and your IRA included. The loss is disallowed and added to the basis of the replacement shares, and when the replacement lands in an IRA it is lost for good rather than deferred. The practical version is easy: give each block a second fund tracking a different index of the same market, and swap between the two. You stay invested for the whole 30 days and the loss survives.

**Where the sale happens.** The account order is its own subject ([[us-taxes-in-the-withdrawal-phase]]), and two facts frame it. Required minimum distributions from pre-tax accounts start at 73 today, and at 75 for anyone who turns 74 after 2032, and they are not optional, so from that age part of the order is decided for you. Before then, the familiar sequence of taxable first, then tax-deferred, then Roth is a starting point rather than a rule: filling the low brackets out of the pre-tax account during the bridge years often beats letting it compound into a bigger forced distribution later.

**Long-term hygiene.** One review a year is plenty ([[the-annual-review]]). Check each fund's tracking difference, since a fund that drifts gets replaced. Watch the assets: a fund shrinking under a couple hundred million dollars ends up merged or liquidated, which is manageable but taxable in a brokerage account. Keep a passive eye on the market, because fees drift down. But do **not** move a fund for three basis points if the sale realizes ten years of gains. Price the tax bill first; it usually wins the argument.

**Construction mistakes to avoid**, in one list: niche ETFs, thematic or sector or single-country, which are bets and not blocks; dividend and covered-call funds bought "for the income", the subject of the opening callout; leveraged and inverse products, disqualified by the daily reset that erodes them ([[arithmetic-vs-geometric-returns]]); and a single sponsor on principle, when two sponsors across seven holdings buy operational diversification for free.

::: exemple The whole portfolio, in seven holdings
Karim and Léa's target ([[international-diversification]]): $1.6M, 65/35, a fourteen-year uncovered phase, this whole book assembled. The **taxable** brokerage (50% of the money) holds the total world fund at 34%, small-cap value at 8%, and the T-bill fund at 8%, which is the buffer and the short sleeve. The **Roth** (13%) holds nothing but that same world fund, because it is the block with the longest runway and the highest expected return. The **traditional 401(k) and IRA** (37%) hold everything that throws off ordinary income: intermediate Treasuries 11%, short TIPS 6%, gold 5%, managed futures 5%, and the last 10% of the world fund to fill the sleeve. Seven holdings, three account types, about **0.12% a year** all in. Each holding fills a named role in the table of defenses, rebalancing at bands of plus or minus 5 points is executed through the withdrawal sales themselves, and every ordinary-income block sits where its income is invisible. That is **all** of it: the sophistication of this portfolio is in its design, not in its line count. And it fits on the written page of the plan ([[building-your-plan]]).
:::

## The essentials

- Every block of this book has a US-listed fund behind it, and the wrapper does real work: a 1940 Act fund with in-kind creation and redemption distributes almost no capital gains, which is the advantage that counts in a taxable account. Income arrives whether you want it or not, since no accumulating share class exists here, and your paycheck comes from scheduled sales rather than from chasing yield.
- Four technical choices: the ETF over the mutual fund in a taxable account, physical replication with three blocks that are not 1940 Act funds and are taxed differently (gold at the collectibles rate, managed futures as ordinary income), a large cheap sponsor whose securities-lending terms you have read, and, in place of the European domicile question, which account each block goes in.
- The real measure of cost is the tracking difference over three to five years, not the expense ratio. A complete portfolio from the table runs 0.10 to 0.25% a year all in, against 1.5 to 2% delivered through an advisor in loaded funds, and over 40 years that gap is worth half a point to a full point of withdrawal rate.
- Six to eight holdings cover everything: world stocks, an optional tilt, the bond core, TIPS, gold, trend, T-bills, each one filling a role in the table of defenses. Fund collecting adds nothing and complicates all of it.
- Sell cleanly: specific identification set before the trade, limit orders in the middle of the session, the long-term holding period and the wide 0% band of the bridge years, the 30-day wash-sale window respected with a paired fund, and never a fund migration without pricing the tax bill first.
