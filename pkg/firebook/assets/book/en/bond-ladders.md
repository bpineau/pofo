# Bond ladders (and the ladder of linkers)
<!-- source: echelle-obligataire @ 112672d1686a -->

There are two ways to own bonds. This book has mostly talked about the first one, the constant-duration **fund** ([[bonds-in-retirement]]). That is the permanent sleeve, the one that absorbs shocks and gets rebalanced. The second is the **ladder**. You own a series of bonds, each held to its own maturity, one "rung" per year of spending to fund: 2027, 2028, 2029, and on down the line.

A ladder is not a technical variation on the fund. It changes the whole **frame**. You stop running a portfolio against a market and start **matching** dated liabilities with contractual cash flows. Retirement becomes an actuary's problem rather than an investor's ([[annuities-and-safety-first]], the same safety-first school, in bond form and reversible). Used well, the ladder is the most powerful tool in this book for three precise jobs: the bridge to your pension, the floor of the early years, and big dated expenses. Used badly, it is a pile of illiquid holdings that rebuilds, in nominal terms, the false security inflation eats away.

This chapter gives the principle, and the one property that sets a ladder apart: why "held to maturity" really does change the nature of the risk **here**, when it was an illusion for the permanent sleeve. Then the three uses and how each one gets built, the indexed version (the ladder of linkers, the closest thing to a guaranteed pension that exists), how to buy the rungs at retail, and the traps.

::: cle When "hold it to maturity" stops being an illusion
For a **permanent** sleeve, holding to maturity protects nothing. A rate rise costs you the same, as forgone income instead of a capital loss ([[bonds-in-retirement]]). For a **dated liability**, everything changes. If the bond maturing in 2031 funds exactly the spending of 2031, the price swings in between are **beside the point**. The cash flow at maturity is contractual, and it lands on the day you need it. The ladder does not remove interest-rate risk. It **cancels** it by matching, which is what immunization means. The risk exists only when the horizon of the asset and the horizon of the need fail to line up. This is the one structure in the book where the word "guaranteed" is literally true.
:::

## The three jobs a ladder does

**Job 1: the bridge to your pension.** The FIRE use case above all others, already met along the way ([[vpw]], [[horizon-and-life-expectancy]]). Between the day you stop working and the day your pension or Social Security starts, there is an **uncovered phase** of 10 to 20 years in which the portfolio pays for everything. The **floor** part of those years is a dated liability: known, non-negotiable, the perfect candidate for matching. Building it is simple. One rung a year, sized to the uncovered floor, from year one to the year the pensions start. The rest of the money, relieved of the floor, is free to carry the comfort layer and the long term ([[stock-bond-allocation]]: covering the floor frees you toward the top of the allocation plateau). Where that last year falls is a claiming decision, not a market one ([[us-healthcare-and-social-security]]).

**Job 2: the floor of the early years (the fragile window).** The same move, shorter. You match 5 to 8 years of floor on the day you retire, exactly the years when sequence risk peaks ([[sequence-of-returns]]). It is the second bucket of the bucket strategy, made contractual and unambiguous ([[the-bucket-strategy]]). And unlike a cash buffer ([[cash-buffer]]), rungs at 3 to 8 years **earn** the market rate. The short ladder is a buffer that gives up almost nothing in opportunity cost.

**Job 3: dated expenses.** Putting the kids through college (2031 to 2036), paying off the last of a loan, a roof you know you will replace. Any liability with a date and a number can be matched to a rung, lifted out of the risky portfolio, and it stops distorting how you size the plan ([[how-much-you-need]]).

What a ladder does **not** do. It does not replace the permanent bond sleeve, since the rebalanced shock absorber is the fund's job. And it does not cover the open-ended **very** long term. You do not ladder 40 years: the distant rungs are expensive against uncertain inflation, and longevity stays open at the end. Past the ladder, the right tools are the portfolio and, late in life, an annuity ([[annuities-and-safety-first]]).

## Nominal or indexed: the question that decides everything

A **nominal** rung of $40,000 maturing in 2038 will fund $40,000 of 2038 money, which is about $30,000 of today's at 2.5% inflation. Past 5 to 7 years, a nominal "guarantee" is a guarantee of **shrinking purchasing power**, the classic false security ([[inflation-and-withdrawal-rates]]). Three answers, cleanest first.

- **The ladder of linkers.** Every rung indexed to prices. The 2038 cash flow will be $40,000 **real**, by contract. This is the "guaranteed pension" object met earlier ([[inflation-linked-bonds]]: a 30-year TIPS ladder funding about 4.5% a year when real yields sit near 2%, about 3.9% at 1%). It is the canonical answer as soon as the ladder runs past 5 to 7 years. The window of real yields in which such a ladder beats the 4% rule, and how much the horizon you pick moves the answer, are on the plate in [[inflation-linked-bonds]].
- **The inflated nominal ladder.** Nominal rungs that grow (40,000 × 1.025^n). It covers **expected** inflation and stays naked against surprises. Fine for the short rungs, steadily more fragile after that.
- **The short rolling ladder.** Nominal, 3 to 5 years only, rebuilt at the top every year. Inflation over a short horizon carries little uncertainty. The long-horizon risk stays in the portfolio, handled by its building blocks ([[defensive-assets]]).

The doctrine that follows is simple. Nominal is fine when the ladder is short. When it is long, it is indexed or nothing.

## Building it, rung by rung

A US investor is unusually well served here: everything the last section described can be bought at retail, in an afternoon, with no gatekeeper to work around. The Treasury sells notes, bonds and TIPS at auction to anyone with $100, and the secondary market at any broker quotes every issue still outstanding, which is how you buy the exact maturity you need instead of whichever one is on the auction calendar this month.

| Vehicle | Useful horizon | Indexed | Cost | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T-bills, money market fund | Years 1-2 | No | ~0 | Any broker, TreasuryDirect |
| Treasury notes and bonds | Any maturity out to 30 years | No | Free at auction, a few dollars at a broker | TreasuryDirect, any broker |
| TIPS | 5, 10 and 30 at auction, any live maturity on the secondary market | Yes | Same | TreasuryDirect, any broker |
| Defined-maturity Treasury and corporate ETFs | One fund per year into the 2030s | No | About 0.07 to 0.10% | Any broker |
| Defined-maturity TIPS ETFs | One fund per year into the 2030s | Yes | About 0.10% | Any broker |
| Brokered CDs | 3 months to 10 years | No | Dealer spread | Any broker |

**Which rung for which job.** Years one and two are not rungs at all: that is the cash buffer, in T-bills or a money market fund ([[cash-buffer]]). From year three out to about year seven, nominal notes do the job cheaply, with a maturity available for every year. Past that, buy TIPS, for the reason the last section gave: at ten years and beyond, a nominal rung guarantees the wrong thing.

**Buying them.** At auction you place a noncompetitive bid and take the yield the auction sets, in $100 increments, through TreasuryDirect or through your broker. Notes come in 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10-year terms, bonds in 20 and 30, TIPS in 5, 10 and 30. But a ladder really gets built on the secondary market, which quotes every maturity still alive on whatever day you choose. One practical detail: a security sitting in TreasuryDirect has to be transferred to a broker before it can be sold, so keep the whole ladder in the brokerage account. Terms checked on treasurydirect.gov in August 2026; the Treasury does change them, so read the site before you buy.

**The hole in the TIPS calendar.** Not every year has a TIPS maturing in it, and there is a well-known stretch of the 2030s with none at all. The standard fix is the shoulder years: buy extra of the maturities on either side of the gap and spread the proceeds across the missing ones. It costs a little precision, never the structure.

**Defined-maturity ETFs, the ready-made rung.** These funds hold a basket of bonds that all mature in the **same** year, then liquidate and pay out. They are the rung in a box, and they make a small ladder practical: one ticker a year instead of twenty settlements. The two big lineups, iShares iBonds and Invesco BulletShares, run one fund per year across Treasuries, corporates, municipals and, more to the point here, TIPS. Check the quality of the basket (Treasury or broad investment grade, never high yield, see the traps below) and the exact month the fund liquidates. That indexed line matters: a retiree who cannot hold individual bonds, inside a plan account for instance, still gets a real ladder.

**Brokered CDs, the insured cousin.** Same shape with a bank behind it instead of the Treasury, insured by the FDIC up to $250,000 per depositor per bank, sometimes at a slightly better yield: a fine rung, as long as you stay inside the insurance limit and accept that its interest, unlike a Treasury's, is taxed by your state.

**Where to hold it.** Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax, worth real money in a high-tax state. TIPS have the opposite problem, since the annual increase in principal is taxed in the year it accrues with no cash arriving to pay the bill: put the TIPS rungs in a tax-deferred or Roth account whenever you have the room ([[us-accounts-and-account-order]]).

**In a simulator.** A ladder is modeled by equivalence. For a matched pension bridge, take the cost of the ladder out of the simulated capital, then enter the covered floor as income ([[using-the-fire-simulator]]: the side income and pension sliders carry the structure). The failure probability left on the screen is then the failure of the **comfort** layer alone. That is the whole point, and the example in [[inflation-linked-bonds]] put numbers on it.

::: attention The traps in building one
**Four recurring traps.** Yield chasing, first: swapping Treasuries for corporates to pick up a point. On an instrument whose **entire** purpose is the certainty of the cash flow, adding default risk defeats the object ([[bonds-in-retirement]]: high yield, never). Then excessive granularity: fifteen separate holdings of $12,000, each with its own settlement and its own bid-ask spread. Defined-maturity funds solve that. Then the ladder as a prison, the whole portfolio laddered "for safety". A ladder has no growth, no room to spend more, and nothing to leave. It matches the **floor**, never the comfort ([[annuities-and-safety-first]], the same rule as an annuity). And the forgotten rung, last. A ladder is alive: realized inflation moves and so does spending. Revisit it at the annual review ([[the-annual-review]]), not only on the day you build it.
:::

::: exemple The bridge Claire and Idris built
Take the couple from [[choosing-your-strategy]] again: a floor of $45,000, pensions in 13 years that will cover about 53% of it. The liability to match is 13 years × $45,000 of floor, less what the comfort portfolio can deliver in any weather. They decide to match 100% of the floor for years 1 to 6 (the fragile window) and 60% for years 7 to 13. The build reads line by line. Years 1 and 2, T-bills and a money market fund ($92k). Years 3 to 8, nominal Treasury rungs and defined-maturity Treasury funds maturing 2028 to 2033, each sized up 2.5% a year (about $220k). Years 9 to 13, TIPS bought for those exact maturities (about $120k). Total cost, about $430k out of $1.7M. The balance, $1.27M, carries the comfort layer on guardrails and then VPW, at an effective withdrawal rate of about 1.8%. Failure of the comfort layer becomes a footnote, failure of the floor is zero by contract until the pensions start. The price paid is the expected return the $430k gives up: measured, accepted, written down.
:::

## The essentials

- A ladder is one rung per year of liability, held to maturity. Matching the asset to the liability **cancels** interest-rate risk, where "hold it to maturity" was only an accounting illusion for a permanent sleeve. It is the one literal "guaranteed" in this book.
- Its three jobs are the bridge to your pension, the floor of the fragile window (a buffer that earns its keep) and dated expenses. Never the permanent sleeve, which is the fund's job, and never the open-ended long term, which belongs to the portfolio and then to an annuity.
- Nominal is fine when short. Long means **indexed** or nothing: a nominal rung at 12 years guarantees shrinking purchasing power. The ladder of linkers is the canonical answer.
- Building one at retail is easy in the US: Treasuries and TIPS at auction from $100 or on the secondary market for the exact maturity, defined-maturity ETFs (indexed ones included) when the ladder is small or the account cannot hold individual bonds, brokered CDs as the insured cousin. Mind the TIPS gap in the 2030s, the quality of any fund basket, and put the TIPS rungs in a tax-advantaged account. And revisit the ladder every year.
- The traps are yield chasing (the outright fallacy), too many tiny holdings, the ladder as a prison that matches the comfort too, and the forgotten rung. The ladder serves the floor; the portfolio serves the life.
