# False defensives: what looks defensive and is not
<!-- source: faux-actifs-defensifs @ a078ad523c28 -->

A defensive asset has one job. Hold up, or rise, when the equity sleeve collapses. It is a demanding job, and few assets truly do it ([[defensive-assets]] reviews the real defenders). But the word "defensive" sells. So the market dresses a long list of products in defensive colors when what they really chase is yield, or when they are just another flavor of equity risk. They pull one number on a brochure down, volatility or average drawdown, or they show a fat dividend. What they do not do is the work: protecting the 5th percentile, the one that sizes the whole plan ([[sequence-of-returns]]).

This chapter is the gallery of the fakes. It goes through the best-sold false defensives, grouped by the reason they **look** protective, and says for each one why the protection fails when it counts. One point of honesty first. Most of these products are perfectly legitimate as what they actually are: a style choice, a bet on yield, a diversifier. The mistake is not owning them. The mistake is counting them as a floor. The thread running through the chapter is a single test, the report card from 2008 (deflationary panic) and from 2022 (an inflation and rate shock).

::: cle The idea in one sentence
A defensive asset is judged by how it behaves in the worst equity regimes, never by a statistic taken from calm markets. Low volatility, a generous dividend or a "real asset" label prove nothing. The only proof is the crisis report card. A false defensive looks good on the sales sheet and does badly in at least one of the two great tests, 2008 or 2022.
:::

## Low volatility is not a defense (low vol, minimum variance)

Low volatility and minimum variance ETFs pick, or weight, the least agitated stocks. They look defensive: measured volatility is lower, historical drawdowns gentler, beta around 0.7. But they are still 100% stocks. In a bear market they fall too, only a little less. A beta of 0.7 gives roughly −28% when the market does −40%. That is not protection. It is a bit less exposure.

There is worse, and it is instructive. The low vol factor leans toward stable sectors, utilities and consumer staples, that trade like bond substitutes. So it breaks down when long rates jump, as in the spring of 2013, which is precisely one of the regimes you were hoping to be defended against. And when panic arrives, it falls with the market. In February and March 2020, US min-vol lost close to a third, same as the index. A style can also lag for a whole decade. The verdict is clear. Low volatility is a legitimate style choice **inside** the equity sleeve, a way to own stocks with a smoother ride ([[factors-in-retirement]]). It is never a portfolio defense. Swapping your bonds or your gold for min-vol stocks leaves the plan fully exposed to the crash.

## Income is not a coupon (high dividend, aristocrats, covered calls)

This is the market's most stubborn illusion. A dividend looks like a bond coupon. It is not one. The capital behind it stays 100% equity risk, and the payment gets cut at the worst possible moment.

**High dividend** funds go after the highest yields on the market. A high yield often flags a company in trouble, a value trap. Beta stays at 0.8 to 0.9, the drawdowns are equity drawdowns, and the dividend gets cut in crises (S&P 500 dividends fell about 25% in 2008-2009). You collect the equity drawdown plus an income that thins out just when you need it. That is the yield illusion, taken apart by ERN ([[the-ern-series]], [[risk-premia]]).

**Dividend Aristocrats** (companies that have raised their dividend for twenty-five years or more) are a better-quality variant. But this is a quality-value tilt concentrated in a few sectors, with a beta around 0.85. The "aristocrat" label is a screen, not a shield. The dividend can still be cut in a deep enough crisis, and the style can trail the index for years.

**Covered calls** (buy-write, popularized by funds of the QYLD and JEPI kind) sell themselves as "income, with less volatility". The premium collected cushions small declines and smooths the volatility number. But the asymmetry runs the wrong way. You keep **all** of the downside and cap the upside. In a −40% crash, a premium worth a few percent a year barely dents the loss. In the rebound, the cap cuts the recovery short exactly when it would be repairing that loss. Economically this is a short volatility position, which means selling insurance. It loses precisely when insurance should be paying ([[long-volatility]]). The "income" it distributes is mostly your own capital handed back to you, plus the gains you agreed to give up. It is a yield strategy, not a defense.

## Correlated to stocks when it matters (REITs, high yield)

These two diversify in normal times and let go in a crisis, which is exactly the opposite of the defensive contract.

**Listed property (REITs)** are real assets that pay an income, sold as a bond substitute. On the exchange, though, they trade like leveraged, rate-sensitive stocks. Their correlation with equities jumps in a crash, and the fall comes with it. Listed property lost about 60% between September 2008 and February 2009, then more than a third in a single month in the spring of 2020, and the rate rise punished it again in 2022. It diversifies in calm weather. It does not defend in a crisis. Property owned directly is another matter, supplementary income rather than a portfolio defense ([[real-estate-in-retirement]]).

**High yield** (junk corporate bonds) is a hybrid of stock and bond. The extra yield, the spread, pays for default risk, and defaults cluster exactly in recessions and crashes, when the correlation with stocks climbs toward 0.7. You get a bond's yield in calm markets and an equity loss in a crisis. What defends in deflationary crises is government duration ([[bonds-in-retirement]]), not high yield.

## Laundered volatility (private equity)

**Private equity**, and private credit with it, now reaches individual investors through evergreen and interval funds. It looks defensive: reported volatility is low, returns are smooth, correlation is weak. That is a measurement illusion, known as volatility laundering. The smoothness does not come from low economic risk. It comes from rare, subjective marks set by appraisal. Economically, private equity is a small-cap stock with leverage, so **more** risk, plus illiquidity and high fees on top. The low correlation is an artifact of the arithmetic. In a real, drawn-out decline the marks eventually catch up with reality, late. Illiquidity is not diversification. The verdict has two parts. This is a bet on returns, and one whose edge net of fees is disputed, never a defense. And its artificial calm can lull a plan into under-provisioning.

## Risk-on in disguise (crypto)

**Crypto** borrows gold's vocabulary: digital gold, an uncorrelated inflation hedge. So it looks defensive to some. In practice it behaves like a high-beta risk asset. It dives **with** stocks in liquidity panics (March 2020), fell more than 70% in 2022, posts extreme drawdowns (−80% is routine), pays no cash flow, and has no crisis report card as a hedge. Its history runs a little over fifteen years, most of it inside a bull market. It may deserve a small speculative allocation for the shape of its return distribution, a debate this chapter is not having. Counting it as a defense is a category error. It is the exact opposite of a floor.

## What they share, and the test

One mechanism links them all. Each one pulls down a calm-weather number, volatility, beta, a dividend, a smoothed valuation, or borrows defensive vocabulary, while leaving the plan exposed to the very risk a defense is supposed to cover: the deep, drawn-out equity or inflation regime that sets the sustainable withdrawal rate. Most of them are legitimate as what they really are. A style choice (low vol, aristocrats), a yield strategy (covered calls, high dividend), a return diversifier (REITs, high yield, private equity), or a speculation (crypto). The error is in the label, not always in the asset.

The test is the one from the neighboring chapter ([[defensive-assets]]). Ask "what did this product do in 2008 and in 2022?". A real defensive asset shows a good report card in at least one of the two, and no disaster in the other. Nothing in this chapter passes. The doctrine that follows is simple. Keep the real defenders for the job of defense: cash, government duration, inflation-linked bonds, gold, trend ([[gold-in-retirement]], [[inflation-linked-bonds]], [[managed-futures]]). And if you own one of these false defensives on its own merits, count it in the return-seeking sleeve, never in the floor.

::: attention The two traps of the moment
Two false defensives are being sold especially hard right now, and deserve up-to-date attention. Covered call income funds (QYLD, JEPI and the clones that followed) attract buyers with a headline double-digit yield. Much of that yield is a return of capital, and the strategy gives up its upside in rebounds, the very rebounds that follow the crashes a retiree has to get through ([[bear-markets-in-retirement]]). Retail private equity (evergreen and interval funds) promises institutional performance with the volatility of a stable value fund. The low volatility is an accounting artifact, the illiquidity is real, and the fees eat the supposed edge. Faced with either one, go back to the crisis test and the spec ([[defensive-assets]]).
:::

::: exemple The "income" sleeve that did not defend
A couple replaces its 20% defensive sleeve (bonds and gold) with a 20% "income" sleeve, half high dividend and half covered calls, drawn in by a 7% distribution yield. On paper the portfolio pays more and looks less volatile. Now replay 2008-2009. The high dividend sleeve falls with stocks, and its income thins at the worst moment, when the index's dividends are cut. The covered call sleeve cushions the fall a little thanks to the premiums collected, then captures only about 70% of the big 2009 rebound, the one that was supposed to repair the loss. The defensive sleeve they gave up would have risen instead, carried by long government bonds and by gold. The high income protected nothing, because it was only a draw on capital that stayed 100% stocks. The crisis test had said so before they bought.
:::

## The essentials

- A defensive asset is defined by its crisis report card (2008, 2022), not by a calm-weather statistic. Low volatility, a fat dividend or a "real asset" label prove nothing.
- Low vol and minimum variance are still stocks (beta around 0.7) and take the crash barely better than the index. A style choice inside the equity sleeve, not a defense.
- Income is not a coupon. High dividend and aristocrats keep the equity risk and watch their dividend get cut in a crisis. Covered calls sell volatility, so they lose when insurance pays, and what they distribute is mostly capital.
- REITs and high yield diversify in calm markets and correlate with stocks in a crash. Retail private equity hides its risk behind smoothed marks (volatility laundering). Crypto is a high-beta risk asset, not a hedge.
- Most of these products are legitimate as a yield bet or a style choice. The mistake is counting them in the floor. Keep the real defenders for defense ([[defensive-assets]]).
